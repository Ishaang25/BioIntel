## Risks

Identify the material **scientific** risks — the things that would cause this
programme to fail for reasons a scientist could have anticipated. Include, where
the material supports it:

- claims contradicted by retrieved literature;
- thesis-critical claims with no supporting evidence at all;
- translational gaps (the evidence tier offered is far below what the claim
  implies);
- target-class liabilities that the retrieved literature raises;
- endpoint or biomarker choices that would not satisfy a regulator;
- statistical weakness: absent controls, absent n, absent p-values, post-hoc
  subgroups, single-timepoint readouts;
- competitive or precedent risk visible in the retrieved trial records.

Each risk must be grounded. In `description`, state the basis explicitly:
what the deck claims, what the evidence shows, and that the conclusion is
BioIntel's inference. Reference the claim ids it derives from. Do not raise a
risk you cannot ground in the material above — a plausible-sounding but
unsupported risk is as damaging as a missed one.

Severity: `critical` if it could invalidate the programme; `high` if it could
force a major change of plan; `medium` if it materially affects timelines or
probability of success; `low` if it is a question of degree; `info` for
observations worth recording.

## Diligence questions

Write the questions a scientific partner should put to this company. These are
the product's most-used output — make them worth the meeting.

A good question:

- names the specific claim, dataset or decision it probes;
- could not be answered by re-reading the deck;
- has an answer that would change the assessment;
- is answerable by the company (not "will this drug work?").

A bad question is generic ("what is your regulatory strategy?"), rhetorical, or
merely asks the company to repeat the deck.

Order by decision impact. Cover the thesis-critical claims first, then
translational and clinical risk, then manufacturing, IP and competitive
position. Include at least one question that would surface disconfirming data
the company holds but did not present — for example about experiments that were
run and not shown, or about the arms and timepoints omitted from a figure.

`what_good_looks_like` must describe the substance of a satisfactory answer
concretely enough that an analyst can grade the response in the room.

Produce 8-15 questions and 3-10 risks.
